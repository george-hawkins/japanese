Official stroke order
=====================

I asked Claude about sources for the official stroke order for the non-Jōyō kanji 舛. Its answer follows.

Japan has never issued an official stroke order for anything beyond the primary-school kanji, and even that one is hedged.

The only official document is the Ministry of Education's 1958 筆順指導の手びき (Guide to Teaching Stroke Order). It covers the 881 kanji that were taught in primary school at the time, nothing more. Its preface states that the orders given are for consistency in teaching and are not the only correct ones, and that other established orders are not wrong. It was never revised for the 1981 or 2010 Jōyō expansions, and the 2010 Jōyō table itself says explicitly that it does not specify stroke order. So a Jōyō kanji outside the school list, let alone a non-Jōyō one like 舛, has no government-defined order at all.

What fills the gap is convention and a few de facto authorities:

- The textbook publishers apply the 1958 principles by analogy to the current 1026 教育漢字, and their orders match each other almost everywhere.
- The Japan Kanji Aptitude Testing Foundation (漢検) publishes stroke orders for every kanji it examines, about six thousand through level 1, and tests them at the lower levels. For non-Jōyō characters this is the closest thing to a standard in practice.
- The major kanji dictionaries (Kadokawa, Gakken, Shōgakukan and others) and sites built on them such as [kakijun.jp](https://kakijun.jp/) give orders derived the same way, and they agree in the overwhelming majority of cases.
- KanjiVG follows this Japanese convention for its data, which is why 舛 in it is written 夕 first and then 一, ㇄, 丨.
