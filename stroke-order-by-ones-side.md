Stroke order for 友, 左, 右, 有 and 布
======================================

These five jōyō kanji all open with the same two strokes, a horizontal 一 and a left-falling ノ, crossed to form the ナ-shaped element that Heisig calls _by one's side_. The rest of the character then sits in the bottom right:

| kanji | keyword | remainder |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 左 | left | 工 |
| 友 | friend | 又 |
| 右 | right | 口 |
| 有 | possess | 月 |
| 布 | linen | 巾 |

The catch is that the order of those two strokes is not the same across the group. Two of them write 一 first, three write ノ first, and the usual rule of thumb ("the stroke that ends up longer is written second") is correct but useless in the moment, because it makes you stop and think. The goal is to make the order automatic, and that means learning the two shapes as two different primitives rather than one primitive with a rule attached.

The split is 2 versus 3
-----------------------

- **一 first: 左 and 友.** The 一 is a short stub and the ノ is a long sweep down the left side. Think of it as a *tall* ナ.
- **ノ first: 右, 有 and 布.** The 一 is wide and reaches right across the top of the 口, 月 or 巾 below, and the ノ is short. Think of it as a *wide* ナ, a lid over a box.

Once your eye reads these as two shapes, the stroke order rides along with the shape. You stop asking "which order?" and start writing "the stub one" or "the lid one". The only thing left to memorize is which two are the stub group, and one sentence covers that:

> **My friend on my left lies flat.**

友 and 左 start with the flat stroke. Everything else in the set sweeps first.

How to drill it so it sticks
----------------------------

Drilling is the right tool for a motor habit like this, but a few tweaks make the difference between a sheet you fill in once and a habit that survives.

1. **Interleave and randomize.** Never write the same character in a block of ten. Blocked practice feels smooth and fades fast. A random sequence forces the retrieval you actually need at review time.
2. **Exaggerate the difference on paper.** Make the 左 stub tiny and its ノ long. Make the 右 bar wide enough to cover the whole 口. Your hand learns the shape, and the shape carries the order.
3. **Say the first stroke aloud** for the first few sessions, "flat" or "sweep", then drop it once it feels silly.
4. **Write compounds, not lone characters:** 左右, 左手, 右手, 友人, 有名, 布団. Context gives the motor memory more hooks to hang on.
5. **Keep it short and spaced.** A couple of minutes a day for two or three weeks beats one long sheet. Spacing wins for this kind of learning.

Why the group is exactly these five
-----------------------------------

Two near neighbours were deliberately left out:

- **灰** (ashes) opens with the same ナ in its Japanese form, but the Chinese form uses 厂, and the character reads as a different shape.
- **在** and **存** open 一, ノ, then the hook of 才 down the left side, so the left half is heavier than in the five above.

The 厂 family (石, 反, 后, 厄, 圧, 厚, 原 and relatives) also starts 一 then ノ, but there the ノ drops from the left end of the 一 instead of crossing it, so the confusion never arises.
