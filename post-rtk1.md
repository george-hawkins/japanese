# After RTK1: what to do with it

You finished RTK1 and now see 撤 as fingers, bring up, taskmaster, but no English word comes to mind. That is the intended outcome of the method, not a deficiency. The keyword was a temporary aid for learning the character's shape. Its place is now taken by the Japanese words the kanji appears in.

## What should happen when you see 撤

**In isolation.** A kanji is a word-part rather than a word, like *-tract* in *retract* and *extract*. Recognising it as a distinct character that you know, could write, and would not confuse with 徹 is the correct and complete response at this stage.

**Inside a word.** The target is that 撤退 is read as てったい and understood as "withdraw" in one step, in about the time it takes to read the English word. Breaking the kanji into parts helps you memorise the word during the first few encounters, and is a fallback when you cannot recall the word as a whole. If you are still breaking a word into parts after weeks of reviews, the word is not yet learned.

## How much kanji-to-keyword recall do you need?

Three different things can come to mind when you see a kanji:

1. **The shape.** You recognise the character as one you know and can tell apart from lookalikes such as 徹. RTK1 built this, and for a kanji on its own it is enough.
2. **The keyword.** You recall the English label "remove". This is useful while you are learning a word that contains the kanji, and not otherwise.
3. **Words and reading.** You recall words that contain the kanji, such as 撤退 and 撤去, and its reading テツ. This is the real goal, and only vocabulary study builds it.

The rule for the keyword is to never drill it, but to be able to reach it quickly, from memory or by lookup, while learning a word. If it comes to mind within a few seconds, use it. If not, look it up; jpdb.io shows a keyword on every word page. Your ability to recall keywords will improve anyway, because every lookup inside a real word strengthens the link. That is harmless, because what you end up remembering is the Japanese word rather than the English label. Heisig's objection was to drilling kanji to keyword in isolation, which trains you to translate kanji into English rather than to read Japanese words.

Once you know two or three words containing a kanji, the words take over from the keyword. You then guess new compounds from "the 撤 of 撤退", with a wordless sense of pulling back or taking away, rather than from "remove".

## Where RTK1 pays off

1. **Telling similar kanji apart.** You will not misread 撤退 as 徹底. If you do confuse two words, name the component that differs and attach it to the meaning: 撤 has the hand 扌 and means remove, 徹 has 彳 and means penetrate.

2. **Less to memorise per word.** To you 撤退 is two known characters rather than two dozen strokes, so the effort goes into the reading and the example sentence instead.

3. **Keywords as a memory aid for new words.** Combine the keywords into a rough English gloss, then check it against the word's real meaning. For 撤 this works about half the time:

   | Word | Reading | Keywords | Real meaning |
   |---|---|---|---|
   | 撤退 | てったい | remove + retreat | withdraw troops, or pull out of a market |
   | 撤去 | てっきょ | remove + gone | clear away a structure or a parked car |
   | 撤回 | てっかい | remove + times | retract a statement |
   | 撤廃 | てっぱい | remove + abolish | abolish a rule or a tariff |
   | 撤収 | てっしゅう | remove + income | strike camp, pull equipment out |

   "Times" and "income" are no help here. The keyword is a unique label Heisig needed to tell every kanji apart, not a definition. Use it when it helps and drop it when it does not.

   For single-kanji words with okurigana, which make up much of Kaishi, the keyword is usually the meaning itself: 持つ hold, 待つ wait, 退く retreat. For those words the only thing left to learn is the reading.

4. **Guessing unknown words in real text.** Meeting 撤廃 for the first time, you can guess "remove + abolish", and the guess is right. 皮肉, "pelt + meat", means sarcasm, which shows the limit of the method. Treat a guess as a hypothesis and confirm it in a dictionary.

5. **Components that predict the reading.** RTK1 taught you to see the parts. Now notice which parts predict the reading. 撤 and 徹 share 育 + 攵 and are both read テツ, so 徹底 is てってい and 徹夜 is てつや. Even 轍 in 轍を踏む, a kanji outside RTK1, is read テツ for the same reason. Each time you learn an on-yomi, ask whether another kanji with the same right side has the same reading. This is what RTK2 teaches, and you can pick it up as you go instead of studying it separately.

6. **Lookup.** You can write any kanji into handwriting input or find it by component, so you can always look up an unknown word.

7. **Writing by hand, if you keep it.** Kaishi tests recognition only, so your ability to write kanji from memory will fade unless you keep reviewing the RTK deck. Keep reviewing it, with no new cards, for now. The daily cost is small, and it keeps the keywords fresh during the months when you are using them to learn words. Drop it later if handwriting is not a goal.

## What RTK1 does not give you

These are the new targets for every Kaishi card. 撤退 stays as the example even though a beginner deck is unlikely to contain it.

1. **The reading of the word.** Learn てったい as the reading of 撤退; do not try to learn "撤 is read テツ" as a separate fact, and do not study a kanji's list of readings from a dictionary. After two or three words containing 撤 you will know its on-yomi without having tried. Without the reading you cannot say the word, recognise it when you hear it, or type it, so do not count a card as known until you have the reading. Learn the two sound rules explicitly:
   - When the first kanji's reading ends in ツ or チ and the next reading begins with k, s, t or h, the ツ or チ usually becomes a small っ, and h becomes p. Readings ending in ク or キ usually do the same before k. So テツ + タイ gives てったい, テツ + ハイ gives てっぱい and ガク + コウ gives がっこう, but 徹夜 stays てつや because や is not one of those sounds.
   - The first consonant of the second kanji often becomes voiced: 紙 かみ becomes がみ in 手紙 てがみ.

2. **Kun-yomi versus on-yomi.** A kanji standing alone or with okurigana usually takes its kun-yomi; a two-kanji compound usually takes on-yomi. 退く is しりぞく and 退院 is たいいん. A kun-yomi cannot be predicted from the shape of the character; the keyword helps with the meaning of such words but not with the reading.

3. **What the word means and how it is used.** "Remove" does not tell you that 撤回 is used for retracting statements and 撤去 for removing physical objects such as signs. The example sentence does. Knowing that the word is "remove + retreat" is not the same as knowing the word; you also need to know what sort of thing is removed and in what context.

4. **Grammatical class.** 撤退 is a noun that forms a verb with する, and usually takes から: 市場から撤退する. For kun verbs you need the transitive and intransitive pair, 退く and 退ける. For adjectives you need to know whether they are い- or な-adjectives. None of this can be read from the kanji.

5. **Sound.** Play the audio every time and say the word aloud. RTK graduates are typically strong readers and weak listeners, because they have been getting meaning from the written form alone. If the card shows pitch accent, learn it with the word.

6. **Recognising words instantly.** In reviews, give yourself a couple of seconds to recall the reading and meaning from the word as a whole. Break the kanji into parts only if that fails, and grade such a recall as hard or again, never good. Instant recognition comes from meeting a word many times in real reading; reviews alone will not produce it. So start reading real text early; graded readers and NHK News Web Easy are good first sources.

7. **Irregular words and kana words.** The readings of 大人 おとな, 今日 きょう and 風邪 かぜ cannot be derived from their kanji, so learn them as single units. A large share of Kaishi is kana-only words, for which RTK is irrelevant.

8. **Correcting the keyword.** Let the words you learn correct the keyword. 生 is "life" in RTK, yet it appears in 生徒 student, 先生 teacher, 生ビール draft beer and 生卵 raw egg. After enough words the character carries its own range of Japanese meanings and the English label fades, which is the outcome Heisig intended.

## Routine for a new card, using 撤退

1. Check that you recognise both kanji, and note the lookalike 徹.
2. Recall or look up the keywords, compose a gloss, and compare it with the card's meaning.
3. Learn the reading てったい. Note that テツ + タイ has become てったい. Ask whether another kanji you know shares 育 + 攵; 徹 does, and is also read テツ.
4. Read the sentence. Note that the word takes する and から, and is formal.
5. Play the audio, say the word, and note the pitch accent if shown.
6. In reviews, expect the reading and meaning to come from the whole word within a couple of seconds. If you had to break it into parts, grade hard or again.
