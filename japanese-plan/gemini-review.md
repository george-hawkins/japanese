# Review of the Gemini conversations

I read all six conversations (16 exchanges). This file records where I agree, where Gemini was wrong on facts, and where I would have answered differently.

## The most important correction: Genki is not printed the way Gemini said

Gemini told you that Genki "writes the vast majority of vocabulary in pure hiragana with artificial spaces", with としょかん, べんきょうする and えいが as examples, and you ruled Genki out largely on that basis.

I checked this against your PDF of the third edition of volume 1, looking at pages from Lessons 2, 3, 6, 10 and 11 and from the Reading and Writing section. This is what the book does:

- **Lessons 1 and 2** are printed in kana with spaces between words and romaji underneath. This is the style Gemini described.
- **From Lesson 3, the Conversation and Grammar section** is printed in normal Japanese orthography without spaces, with small furigana under each kanji. This covers the dialogues, grammar explanations and exercises, and it is the main part of each lesson. The Lesson 3 dialogue has 週末, 勉強, 映画 and 京都, a Lesson 3 exercise has 郵便局に行きます, and Lesson 10 has 世界の国の中で and 有名人. Gemini's examples of what Genki supposedly prints, としょかん, べんきょうする and えいが, are all printed in kanji from Lesson 3.
- **The vocabulary lists** give each word in kana first with the kanji spelling beside it.
- **A few drills** list their cue words in kana. The first conjugation drill of Lesson 3 does this.
- **The Reading and Writing section** at the back uses only the kanji taught so far, so it prints かぞく, ともだち and おふろ in kana. It does not put spaces between words. This section is the book's kanji course, which you would skip.

I also checked your PDFs of the workbook and the answer key:

- **The workbook** is in kana with spaces between words in Lessons 1 to 3. Its Lesson 3 has としょかんで ほんを よみます and べんきょうします, which is exactly what Gemini described. From Lesson 4 it is in normal orthography with furigana and without spaces: Lesson 4 has いつ日本語のクラスがありますか and Lesson 5 has 日本語の宿題はやさしいですか.
- **The workbook exercises** are written for one person: translation into Japanese, written answers to questions, sentence building from pictures, and listening comprehension with audio. I saw no pair work in it.
- **The answer key** covers the exercises of both textbooks and both workbooks, in normal orthography with furigana.

So the kana problem in Genki is real for the first two lessons of the textbook, the first three of the workbook, and the kanji section you would skip. Otherwise it does not exist. Your other two objections to Genki stand in part. The practice sections of the textbook are written for a class: most of the practice pages I looked at contain items labelled Pair Work, Group Work, Class Activity or Role Play. The workbook does not have this problem. Its grammar explanations use its own labels, "ru-verbs" and "u-verbs", but the one explanation I read closely, on verb groups in Lesson 3, analyses verbs correctly as a base plus a suffix (tabe + ru, nom + u), so the framing is less English-bound than Gemini suggested. I did not read enough of the grammar sections to judge them as a whole.

The main plan stays on the Bunpro and Cure Dolly route that you said you preferred. Because you first made that choice with wrong information about the alternative, there is now a second, complete plan built on Genki in [genki-plan/](genki-plan/README.md), and [comparison.md](comparison.md) weighs the two.

## Other factual errors

- **Tobira: Beginning Japanese.** In one conversation Gemini said it "introduces kanji much more aggressively" and "transitions rapidly to natural mixed script". In another it said it "has the exact same kana-soup problem as Genki". Both cannot be true, and I have not checked which is.
- ***A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar.*** Gemini said its examples have furigana. The basic volume prints the Japanese without furigana and adds a romaji line. Gemini also described the introductory essay as covering predicates and "how descriptive adjectives are functionally just verbs", and told you to read "Sections 1–4 (Word Order, Topic vs. Subject, and Predicates)". As far as I recall, the essay's sections are word order, topic, ellipsis, personal pronouns, passive, politeness and formality, sentence-final particles, sound symbolisms and viewpoint, and it says nothing of the kind about adjectives. Gemini appears to have invented a plausible table of contents.
- **Bunpro.** Gemini said N5 has "around 110" grammar points. The public list has 132, and N4 has 185.
- **RTK.** Gemini said that because you finished RTK1 "you understand how Japanese morphology fits together conceptually". RTK1 teaches the shape of each character and an English keyword. It teaches nothing about morphology, readings or vocabulary.
- **Cure Dolly.** Gemini said her model "is essentially classical Japanese school grammar". Much of it is consistent with school grammar, but some of her central claims are her own choices on disputed questions, as described in [resources.md](resources.md). Gemini also attributed to her the term "identifier" for が, which is Tae Kim's term.

## Where I would have answered differently

**Gemini's recommendation changed with each conversation.** In different conversations it recommended Minna no Nihongo with the Dictionary, Genki with Jay Rubin, Tobira: Beginning Japanese, and Bunpro with Cure Dolly. Each recommendation matched the preference in the message it was answering. The individual answers read well, but taken together they show that Gemini was agreeing with you more than it was advising you. The Bunpro and Cure Dolly answer is the one I also think fits you best, for the reasons in [README.md](README.md), but it deserves that scrutiny.

**Gemini gave you a list of resources, not a plan.** Its final answer names six resources and two daily caps. It says nothing about what order to do things in, how long each takes, how reading and listening should progress, what happens at N4, or how you would know you were ready to move on. For someone whose stated risk is lack of structure, that leaves the main problem unsolved.

**Listening.** Gemini proposed Nihongo con Teppei as the listening component from the start. It is audio only and unscripted, and it has no free transcripts, so a learner rebuilding N5 will understand little of it. Video with visual support is understandable months earlier, which is why the plan uses Natural Japanese and treats Teppei as optional extra listening later.

**Reading.** Gemini proposed Tadoku Levels 0 and 1 and stopped there. The plan continues through Tadoku Level 2 to Satori Reader, where you can display all words in kanji and reveal the readings on demand.

**The "parser pass", the inflection grids and the structural Anki cards.** In the long conversation Gemini designed three study techniques: annotating sentences with brackets and role labels, drawing "state machine" conjugation tables, and writing your own Anki cards that ask for grammatical analysis. They sound rigorous, and I think they were generated to match your description of yourself as analytical. I left them out. Writing good flashcards is slow and is a known way for study time to disappear. Bunpro already tests particles and conjugation chains by making you produce them, which is a better test than describing them. The one element I kept is writing sentences by hand, because you asked for handwriting.

**Kanji readings.** Gemini's advice on readings, about sound correspondences with Middle Chinese, was written for a Chinese speaker, because of how you framed that question. It does not apply to someone whose kanji knowledge comes from RTK. For you, readings come from learning words.

## Where Gemini was right

- Limiting new flashcards and new grammar points per day, and warning that review queues can absorb all of your study time.
- Doing reviews first in each session.
- Treating Bunpro as a replacement for a textbook, including the point that it shows which pages of the Dictionary go with each grammar point.
- Watching the early Cure Dolly lessons as a short course before relying on Bunpro's explanations.
- Recommending *A Dictionary of Basic Japanese Grammar* and Jay Rubin's *Making Sense of Japanese*.
- Not making flashcards from graded readers.
